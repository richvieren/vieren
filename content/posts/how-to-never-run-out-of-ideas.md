A few years ago a chips brand emailed me asking if I could make them a viral video.

I had no concept. No pitch. No brief beyond "make it viral," which, if you've ever worked with brands, is the creative equivalent of "make it good."

I told them I'd think about it, closed the laptop, and forgot about it for two days.

Then I ran into my friend Max at a party. We're catching up, he's telling me about his week, and somewhere in the conversation he mentions, almost in passing, that he used to breakdance competitively.

"Wait, seriously?"

"Yeah, I was pretty good. Haven't done it in a few years though."

I'm nodding and smiling, but my brain is somewhere else entirely. Because somewhere in my Moleskine, buried about 18 months deep, is a single sentence I wrote at 11pm one night after watching a Red Bull video:

*"100 breakdancers, one shot, no cuts."*

Three threads. A chips brand that wants a viral video. A friend who turns out to be a breakdancer. And a concept I wrote on a random Tuesday a year and a half ago.

I didn't connect them on purpose. I didn't even remember the Moleskine entry until Max said "breakdance." But the moment he said it, all three threads pulled tight.

We shot the video. It did well.

And the only reason it happened is because I'd written that one sentence down and kept rereading it.

## The System Behind the Accident

That wasn't a one-off. I just didn't realize it was a system until much later.

For most of my career I kept a single list. A Moleskine. Every idea got a line. One sentence, maybe two. No evaluation, no filtering, no "is this good enough to write down." If it crossed my mind and had even a hint of potential, it went in.

The capture part is nothing special. Everyone captures ideas. Notebooks, notes apps, voice memos, the backs of receipts. The world is drowning in captured ideas that nobody ever looks at again.

The part that made the Moleskine work was stupider than you'd think.

At the end of every month, I read the entire thing. Front to back. Every entry. Every page.

That's it. That's the system. One list, one monthly reread, forever.

I've been calling it a back burner, because that's what it is. Ideas that aren't ready yet, sitting on a low flame, waiting for their moment.

## Why the Reread Is the Whole Thing

Your context changes faster than your taste.

You have an idea you can't execute today because you're missing a skill, a connection, a budget, a collaborator, a client who needs exactly that thing. Six months later one of those variables shifts. A year later another one moves. You didn't orchestrate anything. You just kept the idea alive long enough for the world to rearrange itself around it.

The breakdancer video sat in my Moleskine for 18 months. For 17 of those months, I had no breakdancer friend and no brand asking for a viral video. The idea was useless. And then, in the space of one week, two variables shifted and it became executable.

If I hadn't been rereading, I would've heard Max say "breakdance" and thought "cool." That's it. The connection would've died in the air between us because the other end of it was buried on page 34 of a notebook I hadn't opened since I wrote it.

The reread is the retrieval system. Without it, you're hoarding ideas you'll never see again. With it, you're running a context-convergence engine that gets smarter every year.

## The Ceiling

For years this worked beautifully. The analog version. Pen to paper. Monthly reread.

But the Moleskine has a hard ceiling, and I hit it.

A human brain scanning hundreds of entries will miss connections. You'll see "breakdancer video" and think "still don't know any breakdancers" and skip right past it. You won't connect it to the chips brand email because those two entries are 14 months apart and your brain checked out around entry 47.

The capture is perfect. The pattern recognition is where the manual system breaks.

Year one you might have 50 entries. Year five, 500. Year ten, thousands. The number of possible connections between those entries is combinatorial. Two entries from different years suddenly dovetail into something neither could have produced alone. But you'll never see it because your eyes glazed over on the third page.

I noticed I was skimming. Not rereading. Skimming. The monthly ritual that made the whole system work was degrading because the list had outgrown my attention span.

## The Upgrade

I moved the back burner into Obsidian. Same list. Same monthly reread. But with a second pass.

Once a month, after the manual read, I let Claude Code audit the entire list. It reads every entry. Cross-references them against each other. Cross-references them against my active projects. Surfaces dormant ideas adjacent to what I'm building right now. Flags combinations I would never have spotted because one entry is on page 4 and the other is on page 87 and the connection requires holding both in memory simultaneously.

The AI doesn't generate ideas. The ideas are mine. What it does is the pattern recognition I'm neurologically incapable of doing at scale. Three entries, separated by years, suddenly triangulate into something executable.

The output is a shortlist: Execute Now, Combine, Keep Simmering.

The back burner was always a compounding asset. The AI doesn't change the mechanic. It removes the ceiling.

## How to Start

Get one list. A Moleskine, an Obsidian vault, a single file on your phone. Doesn't matter. One list you commit to for life.

Every time you have an idea, add it. One or two sentences. Don't over-write. Don't evaluate. Don't ask yourself if it's good. The evaluation happens during the reread, not during the capture.

At the end of every month, read the whole thing. That's the non-negotiable part. Skip this and you have a graveyard, not a system.

When the list gets too long for your brain to hold, add the AI layer. Import into Obsidian. Point Claude Code at it once a month. Ask it to cross-reference, flag convergences, surface anything adjacent to your active projects.

Start manual. Add AI when you need it. Never stop adding.

## The Long Game

I spent over a decade as a filmmaker. I took snowboard editing techniques into nightlife. I applied high-end book production to outdoor parenting content. Every significant creative move I've made was connecting two things that had never been connected before.

I didn't know I was running a system. I just kept writing ideas down and rereading them. The back burner made it intentional.

Most people spend their creative lives chasing the next idea. The better move is learning to trust the ones you've already had. They're sitting there, on a low flame, waiting for the world to catch up.

And every now and then, two of them find each other.

That's not genius. That's patience with a system behind it.
