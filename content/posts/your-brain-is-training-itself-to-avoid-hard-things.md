You sat down to do the thing. Record the video. Write the sales page. Send the pitch. And immediately your body said no. Something in your chest tightened and you picked up your phone instead.

That feeling has a name. And a mechanism. And it gets worse every time you listen to it.

## The avoidance loop

Tim Pychyl spent twenty years studying procrastination at Carleton University. His conclusion: procrastination is an emotion regulation problem. It has zero to do with time management or laziness.

When you think about doing something difficult, your brain generates a negative emotion. Self-doubt. Overwhelm. Fear that the result won't be good enough. Your amygdala flags the task as a threat. Same alarm system that would fire if someone broke into your house. Except the threat is a Google Doc.

So you escape. You clean something. Reorganize your Notion. Make a coffee. Open Instagram.

And the dread fades. You feel relief.

That relief is the trap. In psychology, behaviors that get rewarded get repeated. Your brain just learned that avoiding hard things produces a positive feeling. So next time you face something difficult, it defaults to the same move. Avoid. Relief. Repeat.

Every time you go through this cycle, you physically strengthen the neural pathway for avoidance. Like a trail in the woods that gets wider every time you walk it. Meanwhile the trail where you actually do the hard thing grows over.

Pychyl calls this the avoidance loop. Difficult task triggers negative emotion. You avoid the task. You feel relief. Next difficult task, same thing. The loop gets faster and more automatic each time.

## The dopamine side

Andrew Huberman explained a related piece from the dopamine side. Your baseline level of dopamine determines your baseline motivation. When dopamine is low, everything feels like effort. When it sits at a healthy level, you can push through friction.

The problem: every time you escape into your phone or a distraction, you get a small dopamine hit from the easy thing. That momentarily spikes your dopamine, then drops it below where it was before. So you end up less motivated than when you started. The avoidance felt good for a minute, then made starting even harder.

This is why you can procrastinate for three hours and feel more exhausted than if you'd just done the work.

## The two disguises

Your brain won't just let you sit on the couch. Sitting there doing nothing triggers guilt. Guilt is another negative emotion. Your amygdala wants to escape that too.

So it disguises the avoidance as productivity.

Perfectionism is the first disguise. Research shows that people who score higher on perfectionism are bigger procrastinators. The fear that the result won't meet the standard keeps you from starting at all. Pychyl's data showed that more perfectionist professors actually published fewer papers than their less perfectionist colleagues, even when controlling for work ethic. The standard became the excuse.

Productive procrastination is the second disguise. You spend the morning reorganizing your content calendar. You research microphones for two hours. You read a book about launching instead of writing the sales page. Your to-do list fills up with checkmarks. But the one thing that actually matters hasn't moved.

Pychyl calls this short-term mood repair. When a real task triggers anxiety, your brain swaps it for a safer task that still gives you a sense of accomplishment but carries zero risk of failure.

I wrote about how all of this shows up specifically in entrepreneurship here: [If You Think Ayahuasca Brings Up Your Shadows, Try Entrepreneurship](https://x.com/richvieren/status/2042594372332450292). Every shadow that surfaces when you try to build something with your name on it, fear of being seen, rejection, imposter syndrome, scarcity, all of them trigger this same avoidance loop. Entrepreneurship is a slow-drip version of facing your demons. On a random Tuesday afternoon when you're just trying to record a talking head reel.

## What actually works

Pychyl's answer after twenty years of research is almost embarrassingly simple.

Just start. For ten minutes. Don't think about finishing. Don't think about the outcome. Just open the thing and work for ten minutes.

He ran a study where he gave 45 students pagers and paged them eight times a day for five days leading up to a deadline. Students consistently procrastinated on tasks they found difficult and replaced them with easier activities. They justified it all week. "I work better under pressure." "I'll feel more like it tomorrow."

When the deadline forced them to start, every single one said they wished they'd started earlier. The task was never as bad as they'd imagined.

Your brain's prediction about how bad something will feel is wrong almost every time. The dread of starting is almost always bigger than the thing itself.

Two specific moves that interrupt the loop:

First, name the emotion out loud. "I'm scared this won't be good enough." "I'm overwhelmed and don't know where to start." UCLA researchers found that labeling an emotion calms the amygdala. Shifts you from reacting to thinking. Simple, and it works fast.

Second, shrink the task until it's stupid. Don't tell yourself you need to write a 3,000 word article. Tell yourself to open the doc and write three sentences. Don't tell yourself you need to record a video. Tell yourself to set up the tripod and press record for ten minutes.

## The compounding problem

Every time you go through the avoidance loop, the procrastination pathway in your brain gets stronger and the action pathway gets weaker. Like a muscle you stopped training.

But it compounds the other direction too. Every time you push through the first ten minutes, the avoidance circuit weakens and the action circuit strengthens. The friction gets smaller each time.

The founders who ship, who post, who launch, who put their face out there. They still feel the dread. They just got better at starting anyway.

Start before you feel ready. You'll never feel ready. Do it anyway.
